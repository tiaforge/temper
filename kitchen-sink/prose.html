Working with light and shadow on a small balcony

Tia Heinrich · November 20, 2026 · 6 min read

A balcony garden gets two kinds of light: a few hours of direct sun and a long stretch of bright shade. Most plants sold as “full sun” cope with less than the label says, as long as the soil stays cool and the pots do not dry out by noon.

This is what three seasons on a north-west facing balcony in Berlin taught me, with the plants that survived and the ones that did not.

Measure before you buy

Spend one sunny Saturday with a notebook. Every hour, note which part of the floor and the railing is in direct sun. You end up with a rough map, and it is almost always smaller than you expected.

  • Railing: 4–5 hours of afternoon sun in summer.
  • Floor along the wall: bright shade all day.
    • Ferns, hostas and mint are happy here.
    • Tomatoes are not.
  • The corner behind the door: nothing grows there. Keep the watering can there.

Water, then mulch

Small pots heat up fast. A layer of bark or gravel on top keeps the roots about five degrees cooler, and you water half as often. Use terracotta only where you can water every day.

The best fertiliser is the gardener’s shadow.

— an old saying, which on a balcony is literally true

A watering schedule that works

  1. Check the soil with a finger every morning.
  2. Water the railing pots until it runs out of the bottom.
  3. Skip the shade pots unless the top two centimetres are dry.
Before you drill into the wall Most rental contracts in Germany need the landlord’s consent for railing planters that are screwed on.

Terms, in one place

Bright shade
No direct sun, but open sky above. Most leafy plants are happy.
Bolting
A plant that flowers too early, usually from heat stress; the leaves turn bitter.
Which soil did you use?

A peat-free potting mix with a handful of perlite per pot. Nothing special.

For tomatoes I added slow-release fertiliser in May.
